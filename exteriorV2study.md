# Your Facade Taste, Decoded: Design Direction and a Claude Code Revision Brief

Your hypothesis is right: the first brief produced generic houses because it treated the facade as paint plus on/off add-ons on a flat box, while all 12 of your references get their impact from **composition** — thick frames, deep recesses, projecting boxes, curves, and light that traces edges at dusk. The fix is not more styles or more palettes. It is a small library of bold "design moves" with real 3D depth, fewer concepts that each commit to one hero move, and dusk lighting as the default view.

## TL;DR

- **Your taste in one line:** a warm, light-coloured house (cream, travertine, off-white, taupe) shaped by one big frame or curve, cut with deep wood-lined recesses, lit by warm lines of light at dusk, and finished with a designed crown (pergola or floating roof) and a designed base (gate, wall, nameplate as one piece). Curves appear strongly in 7 of 12 images; a vertical "spine" and built-in planters appear in most.
- **Why the app looks generic:** the old brief locked the form ("only redesign the outer skin"), so every concept was the same box with different colours. Toggles had no hierarchy, no depth values, and the default light was flat daylight. Your references are the opposite: one hero move, 600–1200 mm of depth, and a blue-hour render.
- **What to do:** give Claude Code a move library (frames, cantilever boxes, curves, screens, floating roofs, light lines) that it may add *in front of* the architect's plan without changing rooms or floor heights; ask for 7 bold concepts shown first as quick elevation thumbnails; make dusk with 2700–3000K facade lighting the default; and flag anything that touches Pune's UDCPR projection limits (chajja 0.75 m, balcony 1–2 m, balconies count in FSI) for the architect.

## 3. Design move glossary

Each term is explained simply, with the images that use it.

- **Portal frame / picture frame facade** — a thick border (like a picture frame) that wraps a balcony, a floor, or the whole front, standing proud of the wall behind. It gives the house one clear "face". *Images 1, 2, 4, 5, 9, 10, 11 (and 7, 8 in boxed form).*
- **Rounded frame / squircle / capsule** — the same frame with soft corners: a squircle is a rounded square; a capsule is a rectangle with fully round ends. *4, 9, 11, 7.*
- **Quarter-circle or sweeping frame** — a frame where one corner becomes a large arc that sweeps up and over. *5, 10, 11.*
- **Angled / chamfered / tapered frame** — a frame or volume with cut-off (chamfered) corners or sloping sides, so it looks like a trapezoid. *6, 12, 8.*
- **Cantilevered box** — a balcony or room that sticks out with no column under it. Its underside is a **soffit** (the ceiling you see from below). *1, 2, 4, 11, 10.*
- **Floating roof** — a thin roof slab that projects well beyond the walls and seems to hover, with a thin dark edge (**fascia**) and a wood soffit. *2, 12, 6.*
- **Crown** — the designed top of the house: pergola (open beams or slats for shade), floating roof, or roof frame. *1, 2, 6, 9, 12.*
- **Layered facade / solid-void composition** — the front is built in several planes at different depths (front frame, middle wall, deep recess), so light makes shadows between them. "Solid-void" means a deliberate pattern of solid wall and openings. *All 12; strongest in 2, 6, 9.*
- **Deep recess** — a balcony or entrance pushed back into the house, usually lined with wood on its ceiling. Reads as a dark, warm pocket. *1, 3, 6, 7, 9.*
- **Curvilinear / soft modern** — a style that replaces sharp corners with rounded ones. **Streamline moderne** is its 1930s ancestor: horizontal bands, rounded ends, smooth plaster.\[2\] *3, 5, 7.* In a November 2025 Livingetc article on Streamline Moderne, a designer named Alex said "We're seeing echoes of Streamline Moderne in the softened geometry of modern furniture, the curved millwork wrapping public spaces, and the integration of lighting that traces architectural forms."
- **Jaali / double-skin screen** — a perforated screen in front of a window or wall (stone, brick, metal). It gives privacy, shade and a pattern of light. A "double skin" is a screen layer standing off the main wall. *1, 2, 5, 9.*
- **Fins / louvers** — vertical (fins) or angled (louvers) blades that cut sun and add rhythm. *1, 3, 12, 2.*
- **Fluted cladding** — panels with vertical grooves, like a column. Grooves catch light and shadow. *7, 8, 2.*
- **Vertical spine** — one tall strip (jaali tower, stone strip, fluted wood) that ties all floors together. *2, 6, 7, 1.*
- **Figure-ground contrast** — light shapes set against a dark background (or the reverse), so the shapes pop. *8, 12.*
- **Cove light** — a hidden LED strip in a groove that washes light along a surface. **Grazing light** — a light very close to a textured wall (stone) so the texture throws shadows. **Wall washer** — an even wash over a smooth wall. *2, 6, 12, 3.*\[3\]\[4\]
- **Integrated planter** — a planter built into the balcony or frame, with plants spilling over. *1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 10.*
- **Designed base / threshold** — compound wall, gate, nameplate, porch and paving treated as one composition that echoes the house. *All 12.*

## 4. Global and Indian creative trends (2024–2026), with example projects

**Soft / curvilinear modern.** Curves are now a mainstream move, not a novelty. In India, **Whispering Curves House** by Suva Architects (Bengaluru, 2024) uses a curved facade on a 2,400 sq ft urban plot: https://www.archdaily.com/1022624/whispering-curves-house-suva-architects. \[5\]\[6\] It shows that curves work on tight Indian plots, not only villas. In Spain, **The Curved House** by RGB Arquitectos (Altea, 2025) keeps the plans orthogonal but curves the outer shell, and "linear lighting elements trace the building's curvature both inside and out": https://www.designboom.com/architecture/curved-house-sinuous-facade-mountainous-terrain-spain-ramon-gandia-brull-slp-rgb-arquitectos-07-22-2025/. \[7\] This is exactly your image 3 logic, and it is the key lesson for your app: **curve the skin, keep the rooms square.** In Brazil, **Curved House** by efcarquitetura (2025) merges the structure with brise-soleil and uses curves so the house does not feel "rigid and predictable": https://www.archdaily.com/1148934/curved-house-efcarquitetura. \[8\]

**Sculptural Indian curves and arches.** **Mirai House of Arches** by Sanjay Puri Architects (Bhilwara, G+2, 2022) wraps the house in scalloped, earth-coloured walls with arches that shade the interior: https://www.dezeen.com/2022/07/20/sanjay-puri-architects-mirai-house-of-arches-india-architecture/. \[9\]\[10\]\[11\] Its "curvilinear punctured envelope" creates a semi-open buffer all around the house.\[12\]\[13\] It is older than 2024 but still one of the clearest Indian examples of a curved outer skin with a climate job.

**Patterned and parametric screens (the new jaali).** Indian studios are turning the jaali into the hero of the facade. **Cielo** by Sanjay Puri Architects (Nagpur, 12 storeys, 2026) uses "sectionally curved screens, which alternate horizontally and vertically across the façade" to shade balconies in 40°C summers: https://www.we-heart.com/2026/02/19/cielo-nagpur-india-residential-project-sanjay-puri-architects/. \[14\] **Screen 504** (Udaipur, 2025) by the same studio reinterprets screened balconies with patterned facades: https://www.theplan.it/eng/award-2025-Housing/screen-504-a-contemporary-take-on-udaipurs-vernacular-architecture-sanjay-puri-architects. \[13\] **J-House** by Malik Architecture (Mumbai, 2025) uses fixed and operable jaali screens on deep verandas facing sea sun and monsoon rain: https://www.designboom.com/architecture/verandah-perforated-screens-malik-architecture-residential-tower-mumbai-03-30-2025/. \[15\] Nuar Studios wrapped a Jaipur house in a red sandstone jali lattice (2024): https://www.designboom.com/architecture/red-sandstone-jali-lattice-jaipur-house-facade-nuar-studios-india-04-10-2024/. \[16\]

**Neo-brutalist / sculptural concrete.** **Nadhyavart Residence** by Atrey and Associates (Delhi, 2025) pairs brutalist concrete with a jaali-inspired "breathable skin": https://www.designboom.com/architecture/nadhyavart-residence-atrey-and-associates-delhi-india-12-12-2025/. \[17\] ArchDaily's review of its most-viewed 2025 projects found houses were mostly "concrete houses that bore considerable injections of greenery", with "wood and stone … common accent elements" and modernist revivals dominant: https://www.archdaily.com/articles/page/155. \[18\] That matches your palette: one main material, wood and stone as accents.

## 5. Creative vs. generic: practical rules

1. **One hero move.** One shape should be readable from across the street in one second (a frame, an arc, a jaali tower, a floating roof). Everything else is quieter.
2. **Clear hierarchy: 1 hero, 2–3 supporting moves, then stop.** Generic designs switch on 8–10 elements at equal weight.
3. **Real depth.** As a working rule of thumb for 3D: frames should stand 450–900 mm proud of the wall behind; recesses should be 900–1500 mm deep to read as dark pockets; soffits need at least 600 mm of overhang to show their wood. Anything under about 150 mm reads as paint at street distance. (These are design rules of thumb, not code numbers.)
4. **Frame proportions.** Frame face width of 300–600 mm on a G+2 house reads as "thick"; under 200 mm reads as trim. The frame should be clearly bigger than the window inside it, with at least one frame-width of wall between frame edge and window.
5. **Material restraint.** One main surface (about 60–70% of the visible front), one warm accent (wood, 15–25%), one dark line colour (charcoal/black), and one stone feature. Four materials, maximum, plus glass.
6. **Contrast through lines, not blocks.** Dark colour works as thin frames, fascias and gates. Large dark blocks only work in a figure-ground scheme (image 8).
7. **Rhythm.** Fins, slats and flutes need consistent spacing (e.g., 100–150 mm spacing for fins, 25–50 mm flutes) and should stop at a frame edge, not at random.
8. **Asymmetry with balance.** Most references put the spine or frame off-centre (images 2, 6, 10) and balance it with a mass on the other side.
9. **Base and crown finish the picture.** A house without a designed top and bottom looks unfinished in any render.
10. **Design the light.** Every hero move should have its own light: frames get edge lines or soffit downlights, stone gets grazing, the jaali gets backlight.

**Common mistakes that make AI and configurator facades generic:** flat planes with materials "painted on"; symmetrical boxes; colour swaps used instead of form changes; all toggles at once; fins and slats with no frame to stop them; glass railings everywhere with nothing behind them; noon daylight with no shadows; no interior glow; and compound walls that ignore the house.

## 6. Practical notes for India (Pune)

**How each move is usually built, rough relative cost, and care:**

| Move | Typical build | Relative cost | Monsoon / heat notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight thick frames | RCC slab/beam edges + brick infill, plaster, texture paint | $ | Very durable; add drip grooves under every edge so rain does not streak |
| Curved frames, rounded slab edges | RCC with curved shuttering, or GRC/GFRC shells on a steel frame; FRP for small pieces | $$–$$$ | Curved plaster needs a skilled crew; GRC is non-combustible and long-lasting\[26\] |
| Wood-look soffits, slats, fins | HPL exterior panels, WPC, or aluminium with wood-grain finish; real teak only where protected | $$ | Real wood needs re-oiling yearly in monsoon; aluminium wood-grain needs the least care |
| Fluted cladding | GRC or HPL fluted panels, or grooved plaster | $$ | Grooves collect dust; choose 25 mm+ grooves |
| Jaali screens | Laser-cut MS (painted) or aluminium/corten sheet; CNC stone; brick jaali | $$–$$$ | MS rusts if paint fails; aluminium is safer near planters |
| Travertine / stone cladding | Dry-cladding with steel clamps, or adhesive for small tiles | $$$ | Seal travertine; use clamps, not only adhesive, on upper floors |
| Large-format panels | ACP (fire-rated core) or HPL on aluminium frame | $–$$ | Insist on FR/A2 core ACP; HPL handles UV well\[27\] |
| Planters on balconies | RCC trough with membrane waterproofing, drainage cell, overflow pipe | $$ | The biggest leak risk in the whole facade |

Indicative 2026 market prices from Indian vendors (treat as rough, they vary by brand and city): ACP sheets about ₹60–₹200 per sq ft, and, per Urbane Decor's 2026 ACP price guide, "₹250 - ₹420 per sq ft for a 4 mm PVDF exterior facade with aluminium framework" installed; exterior HPL about ₹90–₹250 per sq ft for the material; GRC about ₹300–₹800+ per sq ft; natural stone cladding roughly ₹800–₹2,500 per sq ft installed. These figures come from manufacturer and supplier blogs, not independent surveys.

**Pune rules to check with your architect (UDCPR-2020, Maharashtra):**

- Chajjas, cornices and weather-shades may project into the marginal open space by up to **0.75 m**: Reg. 6.7(a) says "no cornice, chajja, roof, or weather shade more than 0.75 m. wide shall overhang or project over the said marginal open spaces so as to reduce the width to less than the minimum" (as quoted by foot2feet).
- Balconies may be **1.0–2.0 m** wide, on any floor except ground, and must not reduce the marginal open space to less than 2 m (Reg. 9.14, per secondary summaries).\[28\]
- **Open balconies count in FSI** under UDCPR (Reg. 6.6).\[29\] So adding a new balcony box can eat into your buildable area. A decorative frame that you cannot stand in is treated as an "architectural projection … only for aesthetic purpose and not used for any habitable purpose", and is excluded from FSI.\[30\]
- Architectural projections appear to be limited by building height (roughly H/20, with a horizontal limit of 0.3–3 m, per an illustrative UDCPR manual; verify with the official text).\[31\]
- An entrance canopy may be up to 5 m long and 2.5 m wide, with 2.4 m clear height and 1.5 m from the plot boundary (Reg. 6.7(d)). Reg. 6.7(k), as quoted by foot2feet, states: "Construction of ottas, railings, barricades, or supporting columns for canopy or porch shall not be permitted within the minimum required front marginal distances."
- Rooftop projections of up to 30 cm are allowed around the building (Reg. 6.7(e)).\[29\]

These clause readings come partly from secondary sources (a Pune consultancy blog and academic summaries). Your architect should confirm them against the official UDCPR PDF and any later amendments.

**Five questions for your architect:**

1. Which faces have marginal open space, and how much projection is allowed on each?
2. Can the cantilevers in the concepts be carried by the current slab design, or do they need deeper beams?
3. Who will waterproof the planters, and with what membrane and drain detail?
4. Do we have a contractor who has done curved plaster or GRC before? Can we see their work?
5. Where will the conduits for facade lighting run, so we do not cut the plaster later?

## 7. Lighting and presentation

**Why dusk renders look so good.** At blue hour, the sky's brightness is close to the brightness of building lights, so you see both the form and the lit details in one image.\[32\] The cool blue sky is also the complementary colour of warm 2700–3000K light, so the house appears to glow.\[33\]\[34\] Midday daylight, by contrast, hides facade lights completely and flattens shallow depth.\[32\] Light Ideas' guide to lighting natural stone says "warm white lighting around 3000K… enhances the natural richness of limestone, travertine, marble and granite", and Vision Outdoor Lighting calls 2700K to 3000K "the professional standard" for residential landscape lighting — a good match for your materials.

**Lighting layers in your references:**

1. **Soffit downlights** in every overhang and recess (1, 2, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12).
2. **LED edge lines** tracing slab edges and curves (3, 6).
3. **Coves** — hidden strips washing a surface (2, 6, 12).
4. **Grazing/wall washers** on stone strips (6, 2). Per QTL Lighting, a grazing fixture "is typically positioned 12” or less from the wall" (about 300 mm) with a narrow beam to bring out texture; NightScenes notes that rough stone may need it about 18 inches (450 mm) away.
5. **Backlit screens and nameplates** (2, 3, 1).
6. **Light slots** — vertical or horizontal slits in pillars, doors, garage doors (8, 10).
7. **Decorative fixtures** — brass lanterns, rattan pendants, sconces (1, 6, 9).
8. **Interior glow** through the glass (all dusk images).

**What the 3D viewer should do by default:**

- Open in a **dusk preset**: sun just below the horizon, sky gradient deep blue to soft orange near the horizon, low ambient light.
- Turn on **facade lights at 2700–3000K**, with a soft glow (bloom) and a warm interior emission behind glazing.
- Use **soft shadows and ambient occlusion** so recesses read as deep.
- Offer **Day / Golden hour / Dusk / Night** as presets, but keep Dusk as the default for concept review.
- Keep light fixtures as real objects with real positions (so the lighting plan can be exported later), not as baked glow.

## 9. Checklist: creative enough or generic?

A concept passes only if it gets **at least 8 of 10**:

1. Can you name the hero move in three words (e.g., "quarter arc frame")?
2. Is there exactly one hero, with no more than 3 supporting moves?
3. Does at least one element project 450 mm or more and at least one recess go 900 mm or deeper?
4. Are there four or fewer materials (plus glass)?
5. Is dark colour used mainly as lines (frames, fascias, gates), not random blocks?
6. Is there a designed crown (pergola, floating roof, roof frame, cornice)?
7. Is there a designed base (compound wall, gate, nameplate and porch that echo the house)?
8. Does the hero move have its own light, and does the dusk render read clearly?
9. Is the composition asymmetric but balanced?
10. Would it still look different from the other concepts if all colours were turned grey?

Test 10 is the most important. If two concepts look the same in grey, they are colour swaps, not concepts.

## Caveats

- I could not see the images; this analysis relies on your descriptions. I also could not trace the original designers of images 2, 3, 4, 11 and 12.
- The depth and proportion numbers in sections 5, 9 and the brief are design rules of thumb, not code or engineering values. Your architect and structural engineer must confirm cantilever depths and frame sizes.
- UDCPR clause readings come partly from secondary summaries; confirm with the official text and any amendments, and with the Pune Municipal Corporation's current practice.
- Cost figures come from vendor and supplier blogs, which may lean low or high. Get two contractor quotes before deciding.
- Renders flatter: dusk lighting, perfect curves and lush plants make any design look better than it will on a rainy July afternoon. Ask the app to show each concept in monsoon daylight once before final approval.
